Fraction of beneficial spins under greedy dynamics in the pure \(p\)-spin model

Question. During a greedy / SSWM adaptive walk, is there an \(N\)-independent function of time giving the fraction of beneficial spins still available?

Answer. Yes. With time measured as flips-per-spin \(\tau = t/N\),

$$\boxed{\,b(\tau)\;\approx\;\tfrac12\,(1-p\,\tau)_+^{2}\,}$$
equivalent to the ODE:
$$\frac{db}{d\tau} = -\,p\,\sqrt{2b},\quad b(0)=\tfrac12$$

Two features of this are exact and derived (\(b(0)=\tfrac12\) and the initial slope \(-p\)); the full quadratic is an effective law, excellent in the bulk and confirmed numerically, with a small correction in the tail. This note explains every piece — in particular why the rate is \(p\) and where the \(\sqrt{2b}\) comes from, which are the two things that were not obvious.

1. Setup and definitions

Pure \(p\)-spin Hamiltonian, couplings with variance \(p!/N^{p-1}\):

$$H(\sigma)=\sum_{i_1<\dots

The flip spectrum is the energy change on flipping spin \(i\):

$$\Delta_i \;=\; \Delta H_i \;=\; -2\,\sigma_i h_i, \qquad h_i=\!\!\sum_{I\ni i}\! J_I\!\!\prod_{k\in I,\,k\neq i}\!\!\sigma_k$$

In your code's convention the walk increases \(H\) (it maximises fitness), so a spin is beneficial when \(\Delta_i>0\), and a local maximum is reached when no spin is beneficial. Define

$$b(\tau)=\frac{\#\{i:\Delta_i>0\}}{N},\qquad \tau=\frac{t}{N}\ \ (\text{flips per spin})$$

Two standing facts we will use repeatedly:

  • Variance of the spectrum. Each \(\Delta_i\) is a sum over the \(\binom{N-1}{p-1}\) interactions through \(i\), so $$\operatorname{Var}(\Delta_i)=4\binom{N-1}{p-1}\frac{p!}{N^{p-1}}\;\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}\;4p\equiv\sigma_0^2$$ (\(\sigma_0^2=8\) for the SK model \(p=2\), \(12\) for \(p=3\).)
  • Random start. At \(\tau=0\) the configuration is random, so the \(\Delta_i\) are symmetric about \(0\) \(\Rightarrow\) \(b(0)=\tfrac12\) exactly.

2. Why the initial rate is exactly \(-p\)

This is the crucial and clean piece. Work per single flip; recall \(d\tau = 1/N\), so one unit of \(\tau\) is \(N\) flips. Let \(B=Nb\) be the number of beneficial spins and track \(\Delta B\) per flip.

2a. The flipped spin itself: \(-1\)

We flip a beneficial spin \(k\) (so \(\Delta_k>0\)). Flipping \(k\) reverses every interaction that contains \(k\), so its own field flips sign: \(\Delta_k \to -\Delta_k < 0\). It leaves the beneficial set.

$$\Delta B_{\text{self}} = -1 \quad\text{per flip}$$

If this were the whole story, \(db/d\tau=-1\) and \(b\) would hit \(0\) at \(\tau=\tfrac12\) for every \(p\). It is not the whole story — flipping \(k\) also disturbs everyone else.

2b. Every other spin gets a kick — and the kick is biased downward

Flipping \(k\) changes each other spin's stability by

$$\delta_j \;=\; 4\!\!\sum_{I\ni j,k}\! J_I\!\prod_{m\in I}\sigma_m$$

i.e. only the interactions containing both \(j\) and \(k\) contribute. Each is \(O(1/\sqrt N)\), so a single kick is tiny — but there are \(\sim N\) of them per flip, and, crucially, they are not zero-mean.

Here is the mechanism. The spin \(k\) was selected because it is beneficial:

$$\Delta_k=-2S_k>0 \;\Longrightarrow\; S_k\equiv\sum_{I\ni k}J_I\!\prod_{m\in I}\sigma_m\lt0$$

The kick \(\delta_j\) is \(4\times\) the piece of that same sum \(S_k\) that also involves \(j\). Because the whole sum is conditioned negative, that piece is biased negative too. The fraction of \(k\)'s interactions that also contain \(j\) is

$$\frac{\binom{N-2}{p-2}}{\binom{N-1}{p-1}}=\frac{p-1}{N-1}$$

and for a sum of equal-variance terms the conditional mean of a sub-sum is that fraction of the total, giving

$$\mathbb E[\delta_j\mid k] =4\cdot\frac{p-1}{N-1}\,S_k =-\,\frac{2(p-1)}{N}\,\Delta_k \;+\;O(N^{-2})$$
Intuition. Flipping a beneficial spin nudges the stability of every other spin downward. The spin you chose was the one whose field pointed "the wrong way"; since it shares couplings with everyone, undoing its frustration slightly re-frustrates all its neighbours. This is a coherent, one-directional push, not random noise — that is why it survives averaging.

2c. How many of the others cross zero: \(-(p-1)\)

A spin \(j\) leaves the beneficial set if the downward push carries it across \(\Delta=0\). The expected number that cross is (density at the threshold) \(\times\) (mean downward shift) \(\times\) (\(N\) spins):

$$\Delta B_{\text{kick}} =-\,N\,\rho(0)\,\big|\mathbb E[\delta_j]\big| =-\,2(p-1)\,\rho(0)\,\mathbb E[\Delta_k]$$

where \(\rho(0)\) is the density of stabilities at \(0\) (normalised to \(1\)) and \(\mathbb E[\Delta_k]\) is the mean stability of the selected spin.

Evaluate at \(\tau=0\) (Gaussian of width \(\sigma_0\)), with SSWM selection (flip probability \(\propto \Delta_k\), i.e. proportional to the fitness gain):

$$\rho(0)=\frac{1}{\sigma_0\sqrt{2\pi}}, \qquad \mathbb E[\Delta_k]=\frac{\langle\Delta^2\rangle_+}{\langle\Delta\rangle_+} =\frac{\sigma_0^2}{\sigma_0\sqrt{2/\pi}}=\sigma_0\sqrt{\tfrac\pi2}$$

The product is where the magic happens — \(\sigma_0\) cancels:

$$\rho(0)\,\mathbb E[\Delta_k] =\frac{1}{\sigma_0\sqrt{2\pi}}\cdot\sigma_0\sqrt{\tfrac\pi2} =\frac{1}{2}$$

Hence \(\Delta B_{\text{kick}} = -2(p-1)\cdot\tfrac12 = -(p-1)\).

2d. Add them up

$$\frac{db}{d\tau}\Big|_{0} =\underbrace{-1}_{\text{flipped spin}}\;\underbrace{-\,(p-1)}_{\text{push on the rest}} =\;\boxed{-p}$$

So the "\(p\)" is literally 1 (the spin you flip) plus \(p-1\) (the spins your flip knocks out). The extra \(p-1\) is a pure interaction effect: at \(p=1\) (independent sites) it vanishes and the rate is just \(-1\); each extra body in the interaction adds one more spin knocked out per flip. The width \(\sigma_0\) — and with it all the messy \(\sqrt{\pi/2}\) factors — drops out, which is why the answer is a clean integer \(-p\).

Numerics: measured initial slope is \(-1.9\to-2.0\) for \(p=2\) (all \(N\)) and climbs toward \(-3\) for \(p=3\) (\(-2.76,-2.95\) at \(N=100,200\)). ✓

3. The full curve and where \(\sqrt{2b}\) comes from

3a. The ODE is just "a length that shrinks at constant speed"

The closed form \(b=\tfrac12(1-p\tau)^2\) is identical to the statement

$$\frac{db}{d\tau}=-p\sqrt{2b}$$

The cleanest way to read this: change variables to \(u\equiv\sqrt{2b}\). Then

$$\frac{du}{d\tau}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2b}}\frac{db}{d\tau}=-p \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u(\tau)=1-p\,\tau, \quad\Longrightarrow\quad b=\tfrac12 u^2=\tfrac12(1-p\tau)^2$$

So \(\sqrt{2b}\) is a quantity that starts at \(1\) and decreases in a perfectly straight line, at constant rate \(p\), hitting zero at \(\tau=1/p\). The fraction \(b\) is quadratic only because it is (half) the square of that linear quantity. That is the entire content of the "\(\sqrt{2b}\)": it is the thing that is actually linear in time.

The left panel of explain_sqrt2b.png shows exactly this — \(\sqrt{2b}\) versus \(\tau\) is a straight line of slope \(-p\) for both \(p=2\) and \(p=3\).

3b. What is \(\sqrt{2b}\), physically? The width of the beneficial band

\(\sqrt{2b}\) is not just an algebraic trick — it is (proportional to) the linear size of the beneficial part of the spectrum. Measure the mean gap of the beneficial spins, \(\langle\Delta\rangle_+\) ("how far above threshold the beneficial spins typically sit"). Along the walk we find

$$\langle\Delta\rangle_+ \;=\; \sigma_0\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}\;\sqrt{2b} \qquad(\text{constant of proportionality holds to a few \% for all }\tau)$$

see the middle panel of explain_sqrt2b.png (measured ratio \(\approx 2.2\), predicted \(\sigma_0\sqrt{2/\pi}=2.26\) for \(p=2\)). So:

  • \(\sqrt{2b}\;\propto\;\) width of the beneficial band.
  • \(b\) (the fraction) \(\;\propto\;\) area of that band \(\;\approx\;\) width \(\times\) height.

Picture the beneficial spins as a little triangular sliver sitting above threshold. Its width shrinks linearly in time (the band edge marches toward zero at constant speed, set by the downward drift of §2), and because area \(\sim\text{width}^2\), the fraction closes quadratically. The right panel confirms the differential form directly: \(-db/d\tau\) plotted against \(\sqrt{2b}\) is a straight line through the origin with slope \(p\).

One-line summary of the mechanism. Selection + the conditioning drift close the beneficial band from the top at a constant rate; the fraction of beneficial spins is the band's area, i.e. the square of its (linearly shrinking) width — hence \(b\propto(1-p\tau)^2\) and \(db/d\tau\propto\sqrt{b}\).

3c. Honest status of the quadratic

What is exact: \(b(0)=\tfrac12\) and \(db/d\tau|_0=-p\) (§2).

What is effective / empirical: that the rate stays exactly \(-p\sqrt{2b}\) for all \(\tau\). It is the simplest law consistent with both exact anchors that also has the right qualitative endgame (rate \(\to0\) as \(b\to0\), because near a local maximum almost nothing is beneficial and each flip is nearly self-cancelling). The \(-db/d\tau\) vs \(\sqrt{2b}\) collapse (slope \(p\) through the origin) is strong evidence that this is the correct effective law in the bulk.

It is not exact in the tail. A drift-only estimate would predict the band width to collapse faster than linearly (giving an exponential-type decay with the walk ending at \(\tau=\ln 2/[2(p-1)]\), e.g. \(0.35\) for \(p=2\)). The real dynamics decays slower late on, because diffusion of the stabilities keeps refilling the band just above threshold (we see the band height \(\rho(0)/\sqrt{2b}\) creep up from \(0.14\) to \(0.23\) over the walk — a pile-up of marginal spins). That refilling is exactly what makes the rate fall like \(\sqrt{2b}\) rather than staying near \(-1\), and it is why the actual walk runs slightly past \(\tau=1/p\) (§6).

4. The underlying \(N\)-independent equation in \(\Delta\) notation

Use one convention throughout this section: \(\Delta_i\) is the actual change in the Hamiltonian \(H\) when spin \(i\) is flipped, and a spin is beneficial when \(\Delta_i>0\). For the pure \(p\)-spin Hamiltonian

$$H(\sigma)=\sum_{I:\,|I|=p}J_I\prod_{a\in I}\sigma_a,$$

the flip energy is

$$\Delta_i =H(\sigma^{(i)})-H(\sigma) =-2\sum_{I\ni i}J_I\prod_{a\in I}\sigma_a.$$

Summing over sites counts each \(p\)-body interaction \(p\) times, so the mean of the whole flip spectrum is fixed by the current energy \(E=H(\sigma)\):

$$\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\Delta_i =-\frac{2p}{N}E.$$

This is the clean \(\Delta\)-notation version of the mean-site-energy identity used in the SK paper. It fixes the center of mass of the spectrum. The drift below is the corresponding one-flip effect on the other \(N-1\) entries of the spectrum.

4a. Drift from one selected flip

Suppose spin \(k\) is flipped. For another spin \(j\ne k\), only interactions containing both \(j\) and \(k\) change their contribution to \(\Delta_j\), giving

$$\delta\Delta_{j;k} \equiv \Delta_j(\sigma^{(k)})-\Delta_j(\sigma) =4\sum_{I\ni j,k}J_I\prod_{a\in I}\sigma_a.$$

Now sum this kick over all \(j\ne k\). Each interaction containing \(k\) contains \(p-1\) other sites, hence

$$\sum_{j\ne k}\delta\Delta_{j;k} =4(p-1)\sum_{I\ni k}J_I\prod_{a\in I}\sigma_a =-2(p-1)\Delta_k.$$

So the average kick to a typical other site is

$$\mathbb E_j[\delta\Delta_{j;k}\mid\Delta_k] =-\frac{2(p-1)}{N}\Delta_k+O(N^{-2}).$$

Since the selected spin has \(\Delta_k>0\), the kick is downward on average: flipping a beneficial spin pushes the rest of the spectrum toward smaller \(\Delta\). If \(w(\Delta,\tau)\) is the density of the selected flip energy, normalised on \(\Delta>0\), define

$$E_{\rm sel}(\tau) =\int_0^\infty \Delta\,w(\Delta,\tau)\,d\Delta.$$

For the SSWM rule used in the data,

$$w(\Delta,\tau) =\frac{\Delta\,\rho(\Delta,\tau)} {\int_0^\infty \Delta'\rho(\Delta',\tau)\,d\Delta'}\qquad(\Delta>0),$$

so \(E_{\rm sel}=\langle\Delta^2\rangle_+/\langle\Delta\rangle_+\). In \(\tau=t/N\) time, the mean kick per unit \(\tau\) is therefore the drift velocity

$$v_\Delta(\tau) =-2(p-1)E_{\rm sel}(\tau).$$

4b. Diffusion from the kick variance

The same kick has variance set by the number of interactions shared by \(j\) and \(k\). With \(\operatorname{Var}(J_I)=p!/N^{p-1}\),

$$\operatorname{Var}(\delta\Delta_{j;k}) =16\binom{N-2}{p-2}\frac{p!}{N^{p-1}} =\frac{16p(p-1)}{N}+O(N^{-2}).$$

There are \(N\) flips per unit \(\tau\), so the variance rate is \(16p(p-1)\), and the Fokker-Planck diffusion coefficient is half of that:

$$D_\Delta=8p(p-1).$$

4c. Population equation

Let \(\rho(\Delta,\tau)\) be the density of flip energies, normalised to integrate to \(1\). The drift, diffusion, and the selected-spin mirror operation \(\Delta_k\mapsto-\Delta_k\) give

$$\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\tau} =8p(p-1)\,\partial_\Delta^2\rho +2(p-1)E_{\rm sel}\,\partial_\Delta\rho -w(\Delta,\tau)\mathbf 1_{\Delta>0} +w(-\Delta,\tau)\mathbf 1_{\Delta\lt0}.$$

The plus sign in the drift term is just \(-\partial_\Delta(v_\Delta\rho)\) with \(v_\Delta=-2(p-1)E_{\rm sel}\). This is the paper's population equation rewritten directly for the energy-change variable \(\Delta\), and with the coefficients generalized from SK to pure \(p\).

4d. Integrated equation for the beneficial fraction

The beneficial fraction is the positive mass of the spectrum,

$$b(\tau)=\int_0^\infty\rho(\Delta,\tau)\,d\Delta.$$

Integrating the population equation over \(\Delta>0\) gives the direct analogue of the paper's equation for \(q(t)\):

$$\frac{db}{d\tau} =-1 -2(p-1)E_{\rm sel}(\tau)\rho(0,\tau) -8p(p-1)\,\partial_\Delta\rho(0,\tau).$$

The three terms are: one selected beneficial spin removed, deterministic drift carrying density through the threshold, and diffusive flux back across the threshold. At \(\tau=0\), \(\rho\) is a symmetric Gaussian with variance \(\sigma_0^2=4p\), so \(\partial_\Delta\rho(0,0)=0\). For SSWM selection,

$$\rho(0,0)E_{\rm sel}(0) =\frac{1}{\sigma_0\sqrt{2\pi}}\, \sigma_0\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}} =\frac12,$$

and therefore

$$\frac{db}{d\tau}\Big|_0 =-1-2(p-1)\cdot\frac12=-p.$$

Because every coefficient is a pure number in the variable \(\tau=t/N\), the whole curve \(b(\tau)\) is \(N\)-independent. Integrating the PDE with \(\rho(\cdot,0)=\mathcal N(0,4p)\) reproduces \(b(0)=\tfrac12\), the slope \(-p\), and the sigmoidal decay (parameter-free). It agrees with the simulation through the bulk and mildly over-holds the tail (its diffusion is a touch too strong), which is the same tail caveat as §3c.

6. \(p\)-dependence and the walk length (the tail)

The initial slope \(-p\) makes the decay steeper for larger \(p\): \(p=3\) falls twice as fast at the start as it hits zero, reaching the bulk floor near \(\tau=1/p\) (\(0.5\) for \(p=2\), \(0.333\) for \(p=3\)).

The actual walk (first time \(b\) hits exactly \(0\)) runs a little past \(1/p\) because of the marginal-spin tail:

For \(p=2\) this grows with \(N\) toward \(\approx0.62\) — intriguingly close to the scrambling drift \(v\approx0.62\) measured elsewhere in this project (worth a follow-up: is the greedy walk length per spin equal to the displacement rate \(v\)?). The quadratic captures the bulk down to \(b\sim0.03\); the last few percent live on the tail that the effective law does not resolve.

7. Caveats

  1. Selection rule sets the constant. The clean \(-p\) holds for the fitness-gain–weighted SSWM rule your data use (flip \(\propto\Delta\)). Uniform choice among beneficial spins gives instead \(db/d\tau|_0=-1-\tfrac{2(p-1)}{\pi}\approx-1.64\) for \(p=2\); steepest descent (always the largest \(\Delta\)) is steeper than \(-p\). The \(b(0)=\tfrac12\) and the existence of an \(N\)-independent curve are rule-independent; the numbers are not.
  2. Bulk vs tail. \(b=\tfrac12(1-p\tau)^2\) is the leading (hydrodynamic) law; it is exact at \(\tau=0\) (value and slope) and accurate to RMSE \(\sim0.01\) in the bulk, but the true walk length exceeds \(1/p\) by a fluctuation tail (§6).
  3. Finite size. \(p=3\) is only measured to \(N=300\); slopes and \(L/N\) are still drifting toward their \(N\to\infty\) values (\(-3\), and a limit above \(1/3\)).

8. Derivation from the Eastham–Blythe–Bray–Moore master equation (general \(p\))

Sections 2–4 built the picture "from scratch." Here we anchor it to the published \(p=2\) theory of Eastham, Blythe, Bray & Moore [Phys. Rev. B 74, 020406(R) (2006)] and show that the entire generalization to arbitrary \(p\) rests on a single combinatorial fact. The paper works in the descent convention (minimise energy, flip a spin when its site energy \(\lambda_i<0\)); its unstable fraction \(q(t)\) is exactly our beneficial fraction \(b\) with the sign of \(\lambda\) mirrored — \(q(t)\equiv b(\tau)\).

8a. The paper's model (\(p=2\), SK)

With \(H=-\sum_{(ij)}J_{ij}S_iS_j=-\tfrac12\sum_i\lambda_i\), the site energy \(\lambda_i=S_i\sum_j J_{ij}S_j\) is half the energy change on flipping \(i\) (\(\Delta H_i=2\lambda_i\)); one flips \(\lambda_i<0\). Their three inputs:

  • Drift (Eq. 1) — flipping \(i\) shifts every other \(\lambda_j\) by \(\;\delta\lambda_{j;i}=-2S_iS_jJ_{ij}\).
  • Sum rule — \(\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq i}\delta\lambda_{j;i}=-2\lambda_i\) (because \(\sum_j J_{ij}S_j=\lambda_i S_i\)).
  • Variance (Eq. 2) — \(\langle\delta\lambda^2\rangle=4\,\mathrm{Var}(J)=4/N\).

Modelling the shifts as independent Gaussian kicks (mean \(c/N\), variance \(\sigma^2/N\), \(\sigma^2=4\)) gives their master equation (Eq. 3), in units of \(N\) flips per unit time:

$$\frac{\partial p(\lambda,t)}{\partial t} =\underbrace{\frac{1}{q(t)}\big[p(-\lambda,t)\Theta(\lambda{>}0)-p(\lambda,t)\Theta(\lambda{<}0)\big]}_{\text{flipping: 1 spin/time}} \;\underbrace{-\,c\,\partial_\lambda p}_{\text{convection}} \;\underbrace{+\,\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\,\partial_\lambda^2 p}_{\text{diffusion}},$$

with \(q(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{0}p(\lambda,t)\,d\lambda\) the unstable weight.

8b. Generalising the drift — one combinatorial fact

For the pure \(p\)-spin, \(H=-\sum_{I}J_I\!\prod_{k\in I}S_k\) (\(I\) runs over \(p\)-subsets), \(\lambda_j=\sum_{I\ni j}J_I\!\prod_{k\in I}S_k\), and \(\Delta H_j=2\lambda_j\). Flipping \(i\) reverses only the interactions containing both \(i\) and \(j\):

$$\delta\lambda_{j;i}=-2\!\!\sum_{I\ni i,j}\! J_I\!\prod_{k\in I}S_k .$$

Now sum over the unflipped spins. Each interaction \(I\) through \(i\) contains \((p-1)\) other members \(j\), so it is counted \((p-1)\) times:

$$\sum_{j\neq i}\delta\lambda_{j;i} =-2\!\sum_{I\ni i}(p-1)\,J_I\!\prod_{k\in I}S_k =\boxed{-2(p-1)\,\lambda_i}.$$

This is the only change from the paper: the coefficient \(2\to 2(p-1)\). The very same "each interaction touches \(p\) sites" bookkeeping fixes the energy:

$$\sum_i\lambda_i=\sum_i\sum_{I\ni i}J_I\!\prod_k S_k =p\sum_I J_I\!\prod_k S_k=-pH \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \langle\lambda\rangle=-p\,E ,$$

which is the identity you flagged. So the mean shift a flip imposes on the population, \(-2(p-1)\lambda_i/N\), is tied to the energy through the shared factor \(p\): the drift is the energy sum rule, resolved site-by-site. The convection velocity (\(N\) flips per unit time) is therefore

$$c=2(p-1)\,\langle-\lambda\rangle_{\rm flip}\;>0,$$

i.e. flipping unstable (\(\lambda<0\)) spins pushes the rest toward stability, and the strength of that push scales with \((p-1)\) and with how deep the flipped spins sit.

8c. Generalising the diffusion — where it comes from

The kick \(\delta\lambda_{j;i}\) is a random variable over the couplings. Its mean gave the convection; its variance is the diffusion. There are \(\binom{N-2}{p-2}\) interactions containing both \(i\) and \(j\), each contributing \(\pm 2J_I\), so (using the model's \(\mathrm{Var}(J)=p!/N^{p-1}\))

$$\mathrm{Var}(\delta\lambda) =4\binom{N-2}{p-2}\mathrm{Var}(J) =4\cdot\frac{N^{p-2}}{(p-2)!}\cdot\frac{p!}{N^{p-1}} =\frac{4p(p-1)}{N}.$$

Each flip delivers one such independent (Markov-approximated) kick to every spin; over the \(N\) flips in a unit of time they accumulate as a random walk, giving a diffusion coefficient

$$\sigma^2=N\cdot\mathrm{Var}(\delta\lambda)=4p(p-1) \qquad(\,p{=}2:\ 8;\ \text{the paper's }4\text{ for }\mathrm{Var}(J)=1/N\,).$$

The third and higher cumulants of \(\delta\lambda\) are smaller by powers of \(1/N\) (since \(J=O(N^{-(p-1)/2})\)), so over the \(O(N)\)-flip convergence the kick is Gaussian — the paper's argument, unchanged. The spectrum width itself follows the same counting, \(\mathrm{Var}(\lambda_i)=\binom{N-1}{p-1}\mathrm{Var}(J)\to p\), so \(\sigma_\lambda=\sqrt{p}\).

Summary of the generalisation. Everything sits on one line of counting: a \(p\)-body interaction ties each flipped spin to \((p-1)\) partners. That gives the drift coefficient \(2(p-1)\), the energy identity \(\langle\lambda\rangle=-pE\), and — through the pair-count \(\binom{N-2}{p-2}\) — the diffusion \(\sigma^2=4p(p-1)\). The master equation keeps the paper's exact form; only these two coefficients carry \(p\).

9. The \(q(t)\) equation and its integration

Integrate the general-\(p\) master equation over \(\lambda<0\). The reinjected mirror \(p(-\lambda)\) lands at \(\lambda>0\) and drops out, giving exactly the paper's balance — now with \(p\)-dependent coefficients:

$$\frac{dq}{dt} =\underbrace{-1}_{\text{flipping}} \;\underbrace{-\,c\,p(0,t)}_{\text{convection}} \;\underbrace{+\,\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\,\partial_\lambda p(0,t)}_{\text{diffusion}} .$$

9a. The initial rate

At \(t=0\) the start is random, so \(p\) is a symmetric Gaussian of variance \(\sigma_\lambda^2=p\): the slope vanishes, \(\partial_\lambda p(0)=0\), and \(p(0)=1/\sqrt{2\pi p}\). The convection term \(c\,p(0)=2(p-1)\langle-\lambda\rangle_{\rm flip}\,p(0)\) depends on which unstable spin is flipped — and \(\sigma_\lambda\) cancels either way:

flip rule\(\langle-\lambda\rangle_{\rm flip}\)\(c\,p(0)\)\(dq/dt|_0\)who
sequential / uniform among unstable\(\sigma_\lambda\sqrt{2/\pi}\)\(\dfrac{2(p-1)}{\pi}\)\(-1-\dfrac{2(p-1)}{\pi}\)the paper (\(-1.64\) at \(p{=}2\))
fitness-gain weighted (SSWM, \(\propto|\Delta H|\))\(\sigma_\lambda\sqrt{\pi/2}\)\(p-1\)\(\boxed{-p}\)your data (\(-2\) at \(p{=}2\))

The clean integer \(-p\) of §2 is the weighted case; the paper's constant-\(c\) minimal model corresponds to the sequential rule. Both are the same master equation — the selection rule only sets \(\langle-\lambda\rangle_{\rm flip}\) inside \(c\).

9b. Integrating to \(q(t)\)

No-diffusion limit (\(\sigma^2=0\)). As the paper notes, flipping + convection alone always cross \(\lambda=0\) at a finite rate, so \(q\) reaches \(0\) in finite time — the dynamics converges. This is the "hydrodynamic" backbone.

Self-similar closure. Through the walk the unstable sliver keeps an approximately fixed shape that merely rescales: its width \(w(t)\propto\sqrt{q}\) (verified in §3b as \(\langle\Delta\rangle_+\propto\sqrt{2b}\)), so the threshold density and — in the weighted case — the convection both scale as \(\sqrt{q}\). Feeding this into the \(q\)-equation collapses it to a single ODE,

$$\frac{dq}{dt}=-p\sqrt{2q} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad q(t)=\tfrac12\,(1-p\,t)_+^2,$$

converging at \(t=1/p\) — identical to the closed form of §3, now obtained straight from the EBBM equation with the general-\(p\) coefficients. (For the sequential rule the same closure gives the same quadratic shape with closing rate \(1+2(p-1)/\pi\) in place of \(p\).)

9c. The late-time tail (the paper's actual point)

The quadratic is the leading behaviour; diffusion controls the endgame. As the sliver narrows, its slope at \(\lambda=0\) rises until the diffusive inflow \(\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\partial_\lambda p(0)\) nearly cancels the \(-1\) flipping loss. The decay of \(q\) then stalls once \(p(0,t)\!\sim\!1/N\) (intercept \(\sim 2/N\)), and the finite system converges. That is the marginal-spin pile-up of §3c and the reason the real walk runs slightly past \(t=1/p\) (§6) — precisely EBBM's mechanism for the small \(p(0)\) and the failure of the Edwards hypothesis, here carried to general \(p\).

Bottom line

The fraction of beneficial spins is an \(N\)-independent function of \(\tau=t/N\): it starts at \(\tfrac12\), decays with the exact initial rate \(-p\) (one spin you flip, plus \(p-1\) you knock out), and follows \(b\approx\tfrac12(1-p\tau)^2\) — the square of a band width that closes at constant speed \(p\) — down to a small fluctuation tail near \(\tau\approx1/p\).
